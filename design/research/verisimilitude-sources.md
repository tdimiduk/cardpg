# I. Modeling Armor, Weapons, and Combat Techniques

This category includes information on the effectiveness, construction, and historical context of different armor types.

* **Primary Academic/Museum Sources:**
  * **A. Williams, "The Knight and the Blast Furnace"**: Cited for quantitative data on the energy (in joules) required to penetrate different types of armor, particularly the surprising effectiveness of gambesons against cuts.
  * **The Wallace Collection, London**: Referenced for information on Gothic-style armor.
  * **The Metropolitan Museum of Art, "Arms and Armor in Medieval Europe"**: A general source for the evolution of armor from the early Middle Ages through the Renaissance.
  * ***Fechtbücher*** **(Combat Manuals) of Fiore dei Liberi, Hans Talhoffer, etc.**: Th primary sources for understanding the specific techniques used in armored combat (*Harnischfechten*), including grappling (`Ringen`), half-swording, and targeting gaps.
* **Online Historical Compilations & Blogs:**
  * **TheCollector.com**: For articles on the evolution of armor from maille to plate.
  * **A Collection of Unmitigated Pedantry (acoup.blog)**: For detailed analysis and myth-busting regarding the effectiveness of different armor types, particularly gambesons.
  * **Chivalry Today (chivalrytoday.com)**: For articles on the evolution of armor through different medieval periods.
  * **myArmoury.com & r/AskHistorians, r/ArmsandArmor**: Community forums and subreddits used to find discussions from historical enthusiasts and amateur experts, particularly regarding gambesons vs. blunt trauma and the effectiveness of swords.

# Modeling the Physical Costs of Effort

This category covers the real-world physical impact of combat and sustained alertness.

* **Primary Scientific Journals & Concepts:**
  * ***Proceedings of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences***: Specifically for the 2011 study on the metabolic cost of locomotion in medieval armor, which provided the "more than double the energy cost" statistic and highlighted the respiratory strain of wearing a helmet.
  * **ATP-PCr (Phosphagen) System**: The core physiological concept used to analyze the fatigue from short, explosive bursts of activity (like a 20-second combat round).
  * **Anaerobic Glycolysis**: The physiological concept for analyzing fatigue from sustained, high-intensity work lasting around a minute (like the "archer" or "kata" scenario).
* **Modern Military & Psychological Research:**
  * **Studies on Soldier Load and Metabolic Cost**: Research on modern infantry used as an analogue for understanding the energy expenditure of carrying heavy gear for long periods.
  * **Research on "Vigilance Decrement"**: The psychological principle explaining the degradation of attention and perception during long periods of high-alertness (like a 6-hour patrol).
  * **Studies on Stress Hormones (Cortisol, Adrenaline)**: Used to analyze the physiological cost of hypervigilance and the "crash" that follows a prolonged period of stress.

# Modeling Injury, Damage, and Lasting Harm

This category informed our discussion on damage types and the effects of injuries.

* **Core Medical Principles:**
  * **Penetrating vs. Blunt Force Trauma**: The fundamental distinction used in modern emergency medicine to classify injuries. This was the basis for our initial proposal to move beyond the classic RPG damage types.
  * **Hemorrhage, Organ Damage, Infection**: The three primary concerns of penetrating trauma.
  * **Skeletal Damage, Internal Organ Damage, Neurological Trauma**: The three primary concerns of blunt force trauma.
* **Modern Analogues for Weapon Effects:**
  * **Shark-Diving Suits**: Used as a modern analogue for the effectiveness of maille against biting attacks.
  * **Faraday Cages**: Used to explain the protective effect of metal armor against lightning strikes.

# Document Purpose

This is a designer-facing document intended for internal use. It serves as a living list and a starting point for our factual research. It contains an initial collection of high-quality historical, medical, and scientific sources that model the *kind* of evidence we should use to ground our design in "Casual Realism." Its purpose is to provide a shared factual basis for our mechanics and to grow over time as we discover new, relevant sources.
